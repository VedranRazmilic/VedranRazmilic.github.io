Canadian Content Regulations and Broadcasting: Evidence from the 1999 CanCon Expansion
[with Co-author Name]  |  [Month Year] draft
PDF
Abstract

Content quotas mandate exposure to domestic cultural goods but leave intermediaries to decide which goods receive it. I study how Canadian radio broadcasters allocated the exposure added by the 1999 expansion of the Canadian content quota, which raised the weekly floor from 30 to 35 percent and extended it to weekday daytime hours. Linking digitized Canadian radio charts for 1994–2000 to a music catalogue built from MusicBrainz and Discogs, I compare Canadian and non-Canadian artists before and after the expansion. The quarterly probability that an active Canadian artist charts falls by 1.7 percentage points, 40 percent of its baseline, while the chart slots held by Canadian artists do not change, so the mandated exposure is concentrated among fewer artists. Canadian chart runs lengthen by 3 issues, 23 percent, entirely after their peak and in the middle and lower thirds of the charts, and charting Canadian songs are older. The pre-policy relationship between Canadian chart runs and US chart success would imply an 11 percentage point increase in US charting; after the expansion this relationship weakens and the implied effect is small and imprecise. The results indicate that broadcasters met the quota by extending the life of proven songs rather than by broadening exposure to Canadian music.
